#7 Not just maps: how school atlases shape understanding of climate

Author(s): Vit Vozenilek, Radim Tolasz

Speaker Biographies

Dr. Vít Voženílek

prof. Dr. Vít Voženílek is Professor of Cartography and Geoinformatics at Palacký University Olomouc (Czech Republic) and UMCS Lublin (Poland). He has published dozens of papers on atlases and geovisualization and compiled 16 thematic atlases. He served as Vice-President of the International Cartographic Association for eight years.

Dr. Radim Tolasz

Dr. Radim Tolasz is a researcher at the Czech Hydrometeorological Institute. He specializes in climatological time series, databases, and climate change. He represents the Czech Republic on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and has collaborated with the World Meteorological Organization for several decades.

School atlases remain important educational tools, but their climate parts may strongly influence whether students understand climate as a static spatial pattern or as a dynamic system affected by variability, change, and risk. This issue is relevant because climate knowledge requires more than knowledge of temperature, precipitation, and climate zones. The aim is to compare how climate is presented in selected school atlases from different countries and to assess their cartographic, climatological, and didactic potential. The study addresses three research questions: which climate topics and cartographic methods are used; how strongly atlases reproduce a traditional, static concept of climate versus recent topics such as climate change and hazards; and what types of atlas-based climate representation can be identified. The research is based on a comparative content analysis of 13 school atlases published between 1996 and 2026. Single- and double-page climate spreads were examined using a binary content matrix that recorded the presence of 39 topics grouped into ten thematic groups. Cartographic methods were also classified, and the atlases were typified according to thematic breadth, multimodality, and inclusion of environmental issues. The results show that traditional climatological content dominates: temperature, precipitation, pressure, atmospheric circulation, ocean currents, and climate classifications are the most frequent topics. Only two atlases explicitly include climate change, emissions, hazards, or environmental impacts. The study proposes a typology of climate representation in school atlases and contributes a transparent framework for evaluating atlas content, supporting future atlas design and geography education.
